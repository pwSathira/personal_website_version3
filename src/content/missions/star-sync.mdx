A satellite can only talk to the ground while it's above a station's horizon, which for a low orbit means a pass of about ten minutes, a few times a day. Star-Sync takes requests for that time from satellite missions and books them across a network of ground stations.

There are two kinds of request. A **contact request** books one specific pass at one station, whole or not at all. An **RF time request** asks for some number of seconds anywhere in a window, spread over a minimum number of passes, and leaves the scheduler to pick the stations. Every booking has to obey four rules: a station tracks one satellite at a time, it needs five minutes between contacts to reconfigure, the satellite stays above the station's elevation mask for the whole booking, and no satellite transmits while an interfering satellite sits inside its exclusion cone.

## Measured against skyfield, the old scheduler said yes to requests it couldn't keep

In November 2026 I came back to the project and benchmarked the scheduler against skyfield, which computes when each satellite is actually overhead. The workload was 6 satellites, 3 stations and 150 requests over 24 hours.

The new scheduler books each request for at most its requested time plus one second per pass. Its numbers come from the test suite, which generates a workload, runs the scheduler and checks every booking against skyfield.

I kept the scheduler greedy. An integer-programming solver could squeeze out a few more requests, but the measured problems were correctness problems, and a greedy pass over batched orbital maths places 150 requests in under 100 ms. On a generated workload close to capacity (3 satellites, 3 stations, 50 RF and 27 contact requests, one exclusion cone) it fulfils 52% of requests in about 70 ms, every one of them valid.

Two smaller decisions came out of reading the spec closely. Uplink, telemetry and science data share a pass, so an RF request needs the longest of the three durations, not their sum. And the same rework locked down the API: until then, an unauthenticated `GET /hello/initdb` could drop every table.

The data had to look live on any day someone opens it. Day 0 of the snapshot is always today in UTC, so a pass that was "in two days" yesterday is still in two days. Orbits don't repeat daily, so each satellite's TLE moves with the data: the epoch advances a day at a time and the right ascension of the node turns 0.9856° a day to keep the ground track under the same clock time. A test moves a TLE 200 days and checks that the passes still land within 2 seconds.

Changes a visitor makes live in `localStorage` and reset when the UTC day changes.

## The scheduler still ignores station load

The spec's third objective is to use ground stations evenly, and the scheduler doesn't try yet; it takes the first station that fits. It also meets `min_passes` by spreading time evenly rather than treating it as a goal. Both are next on the roadmap, ahead of hooking up the ground-station simulator so booked passes get reserved at the station.
